Why lead exists: actual and perceived lead

The shot needs a fraction of a second to reach the target, and in that time the target moves on. A gun pointed straight at a crosser therefore shoots behind it. The gun has to point ahead, so that shot and target meet. That gap is lead, in British English also “forward allowance”.

Lead grows with the target's speed, its distance and its angle. A full crosser needs the most; a target flying straight at or away from the shooter needs almost no sideways lead, though rising and falling targets need vertical lead (above or below).

Real numbers are surprising. One shooting website gives about 3 to 4 feet, roughly a metre, of actual lead for a crosser at about 21–25 yards (around 20 m). Many shooters say it grows roughly in proportion with distance, so the same small gap at the muzzle means much more real lead on a far target.

Actual lead is the real distance in front of the clay in the air. Perceived lead (the “lead picture” or “sight picture”) is the gap the shooter sees between muzzle and target. A metre at 20 m looks like a few centimetres at the muzzle. The method changes the picture: of the methods with a moving gun, maintained lead shows the biggest gap and swing-through little or none, because gun speed adds lead. With ambush the gun is still, so the shooter must see an even bigger gap: the target keeps moving during the reaction time.

An American instructor notes that a right-handed shooter often feels a left-to-right crosser needs more lead than a right-to-left one; the real lead is the same, only perception differs. So a coach's “four feet” describes his picture, not a measurement.

Key points
  • Lead exists because the target moves while the shot travels.
  • More speed, distance and angle mean more lead.
  • Actual lead is metres in the air at the target; at the muzzle the picture is only centimetres, because the muzzle is close to the eye.
  • The method changes the picture: with a moving gun, maintained shows the most and swing-through the least; with ambush (gun still) the gap is even bigger.
  • A coach's number is a picture, not a measurement.

The coach says

“Forget the real distance — what matters is your picture.”
Do not think in metres in the air; remember the gap you saw at the muzzle when you hit it.
“That's the picture — remember it.”
The lead was right on that hit; keep the same sight picture.
“Same lead on the right-to-left, it only looks different.”
The real lead is equal in both directions; only perception changes.

Check yourself

What is perceived lead?
  1. The real distance in front of the clay in the air
  2. The gap the shooter sees between muzzle and target
  3. The lead written on the target menu
  4. The distance from the stand to the trap
Correct!

The gap the shooter sees between muzzle and target — Perceived lead is the picture at the muzzle; actual lead is the real distance in the air and is much larger.

Which method usually gives the largest perceived lead?
  1. Swing-through
  2. Pull-away
  3. Maintained lead
  4. All are identical
Correct!

Maintained lead — With maintained lead the gun moves at the target's speed and gun speed adds nothing, so the shooter must see the whole gap.

Which target needs almost no sideways lead?
  1. A target going straight away
  2. A long crosser
  3. A quartering-away target at 45°
  4. A crosser at 40 m
Correct!

A target going straight away — A straight going-away target has no sideways movement; any lead it needs is vertical.
