Behind, in front, over, under, chipped

Every miss has a direction. A coach watching from behind the shooter usually says first where the shot went compared with the clay, and only then why. Pass it on instantly: the shooter is already preparing for the next target.

Behind means the shot passed behind a moving target: not enough lead, or a gun that slowed down. It is the most common miss on crossers. In front is the opposite: too much lead, often after the shooter over-corrected. Many coaches like a miss in front: it shows the gun kept moving.

Over (also 'high', 'over the top') and under ('low') describe the vertical miss. Over is common on dropping targets, rabbits and incomers, and is often linked to the head coming off the stock. Under is typical on rising targets that are still climbing at the shot.

Chipped means only a small piece came off. For the coach it is half praise, half correction: the clay was on the edge of the pattern. For the referee it is a hit, as long as the piece is visible. The trap is dust: under the rules, a clay that gives off only dust or smoke, with no visible piece, is lost. Yet 'smoked it' in coach slang is a perfect, centred hit, and 'dusted it' is heard in both senses, so ask when unsure.

Keep the coach's order: direction, cause, fix. If the coach says 'behind, you stopped', the cause matters more: a shooter who hears only 'behind' will add lead, which does not cure a stopped gun.

Key points
  • Pass on the direction first: behind, in front, over, under.
  • Behind = too little lead or a slowing gun; in front = too much lead.
  • A visible chip is a hit for the referee; dust or smoke only is lost.
  • 'Smoked it' is coach praise, not the 'smoke' of the rules: never mix them up.
  • Keep the coach's order: direction, cause, fix.

The coach says

“Behind, give it more.”
The shot passed behind the clay; increase the lead.
“Just over the top, it was already dropping.”
The shot went above because the clay was falling; shoot lower and in front.
“In front this time, good, now a touch less.”
Too much lead, but the gun was moving; reduce the lead slightly.
“You just chipped it, a little more in front.”
Hit on the edge of the pattern; a bit more lead centres it.
“Smoked it! Same again.”
A perfect centred hit (slang, not the rules' 'smoke'); repeat exactly.
“Behind, but you stopped, so don't just add lead.”
The cause is a stopped gun; more lead alone will not help.

Check yourself

The coach calls: 'Behind, you stopped!' What is the most important part to translate?
  1. The cause: the gun stopped
  2. Only the direction 'behind'
  3. Nothing: the shooter saw the miss
  4. A request to add a lot more lead
Correct!

The cause: the gun stopped — Adding lead does not cure a stopped gun; the cause is what the shooter has to fix.

A small but clearly visible piece breaks off the clay. The coach says 'just chipped it'. How does the referee score it?
  1. Lost, because most of the clay flew on
  2. No Bird
  3. Hit (one)
  4. Hit only if the coach agrees
Correct!

Hit (one) — At least one visible piece makes the target a hit in both Sporting™ and Compak® Sporting; the referee decides alone.

After a hit the coach shouts 'Smoked it!'. What does he mean?
  1. Only dust came off, so it is lost
  2. A perfect, centred hit
  3. It was a flash target
  4. The shooter should change cartridges
Correct!

A perfect, centred hit — In coach slang 'smoked it' is praise for a clay turned to dust; in the rules, dust or smoke without a visible piece is a miss.
