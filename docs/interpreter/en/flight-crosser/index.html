The crosser: a target flying across

A crosser flies across in front of the shooter, roughly at a right angle to the line of sight: left to right (written L→R) or right to left (R→L). On a Parcours de Chasse – Sporting™ layout the rules ask for a mix of crossing, quartering, incoming and going-away targets. In Compak® Sporting a left-to-right and a right-to-left crosser are two of the three compulsory trajectories, so every Compak has them.

A crosser shows you its full sideways speed, so it needs the most lead (forward allowance) of all presentations, and the further away it is, the more lead it needs. A “long crosser” means a far one, not one that flies for a long time.

Read its line and speed: does it fly flat, climb, or already drop at the break point? Does it show its face (the dome or the belly) or only its edge? Does it slow down? A slowing crosser needs less lead than one at full speed.

Typical plan: feet set to the break point, eyes picking it up early, then pull-away or maintained lead, with the gun still moving at the shot. A very common coach's verdict on a crosser is “behind”: not enough lead, or a gun that stopped.

Directions are given from the shooter's point of view. If you stand facing the shooter, their “left to right” is your right to left, so never turn the words round.

Key points
  • A crosser flies across at about 90° to the line of sight: L→R or R→L.
  • It needs the most lead; more distance means more lead.
  • “Long crosser” = far away, not long-lasting.
  • Compak® Sporting always has an L→R and an R→L crosser.
  • Left and right are always from the shooter's point of view.

The coach says

“Long crosser, right to left — trust the lead and keep the gun moving.”
The target is far away and flies from the shooter's right to left; give it the planned lead and do not stop the swing.
“You're behind it — give it more.”
The shot passed behind the target: more lead is needed.
“It's dropping at your break point — stay under the line.”
Where the shooter breaks it, the crosser is already falling, so the gun must travel slightly below its path.
“Pick it up early, it's quick off the trap.”
Look for the target closer to the trap than usual, because it leaves the trap fast.

Check yourself

Which presentation usually needs the most lead?
  1. A target going straight away
  2. An incomer flying straight at the shooter
  3. A crosser at the same distance
  4. A rabbit rolling straight away
Correct!

A crosser at the same distance — A crosser shows its full sideways speed, so the gun must be furthest in front; straight-line targets need little sideways lead.

The coach says “long crosser”. What does “long” mean?
  1. The target is far away
  2. The target flies for a long time
  3. The target is a long, oval clay
  4. The shooter should wait a long time
Correct!

The target is far away — In coaching “long” describes distance. A long crosser is a far crosser, which needs more lead.

What is true of crossers in Compak® Sporting?
  1. Crossers are not allowed on a Compak
  2. Only right-to-left crossers are allowed
  3. Crossers may only be thrown as rabbits
  4. An L→R and an R→L crosser are compulsory
Correct!

An L→R and an R→L crosser are compulsory — The Compak® Sporting rules make three trajectories compulsory: an L→R crosser, an R→L crosser and a going-away target.
