Numbers and units: feet, yards, gauges, shot sizes

British and American coaches measure lead in feet: “give it three feet”, “a yard in front”, “half a bird”, “a barrel’s width”. This is a picture, not a measurement. Translate the unit as said, «три фути», and do not convert it in the middle of an instruction: 0.9 m sounds precise and changes the meaning. Explain once, before the session, that coaches’ feet are an impression, and keep “more”, “less”, “double it” exactly as said.

Distances to targets are another matter. “It’s about thirty yards” can be given in metres with “about” («приблизно 27 метрів»), because a yard is a little less than a metre. Directions often come as a clock face: straight ahead is 12, so “pick it up at eleven o’clock” is slightly left. Keep the clock; do not turn it into degrees.

Gauge works backwards. A “12-bore” or “12 gauge” is not 12 mm; the smaller the number, the wider the barrel, so a 20 gauge is thinner. Both FITASC rulebooks allow calibre 12 at most, with barrels of at least 66 cm (26 inches). The vest line in Parcours de Chasse – Sporting™ is 25 cm, which British shooters may call just under ten inches.

Cartridges: FITASC allows at most 28 g of lead, close to the “one-ounce load” British shooters mention, with shot of 2.0 to 2.5 mm. Shot numbers are not the same in every country: a No. 9 is about 2 mm almost everywhere, but other numbers can differ slightly between British, American and continental systems. When it matters, ask for millimetres. Chokes may come as names, fractions or “thou” (thousandths of an inch); translate the system the coach used.

Key points
  • Lead in feet is a picture: keep the unit, never convert mid-instruction
  • Target distance: yards may become “about … metres”
  • Clock face for directions: 12 = straight ahead
  • 12 gauge ≠ 12 mm; FITASC max calibre 12, barrels ≥ 66 cm (26 in)
  • 28 g ≈ one ounce; shot 2.0–2.5 mm; shot numbers differ by country — ask for mm

The coach says

“Give it three feet.”
The lead picture the coach wants; a perceived gap, not a measured distance.
“Half that.”
Use half of the previous lead.
“Pick it up at eleven o’clock.”
Look for the target slightly left of straight ahead.
“It’s about forty yards out.”
The target is roughly 37 metres away.
“Put a nine in the bottom barrel.”
Load a No. 9 shot cartridge (about 2 mm) in the lower barrel, which usually fires first.

Check yourself

The coach says “Give it four feet.” What is the best interpretation?
  1. «Винос — приблизно 1,22 метра»
  2. «Винос — чотири фути»
  3. «Стріляйте на чотири метри далі»
  4. «Чотири мішені вперед»
Correct!

«Винос — чотири фути» — Lead in feet is a picture; converting it mid-instruction adds false precision and changes the message.

What is the largest calibre allowed in FITASC Sporting™ and Compak®?
  1. 10
  2. 16
  3. 20
  4. 12
Correct!

12 — Both rulebooks allow calibre 12 at most; a smaller gauge number means a wider bore, so 10 is not allowed.

A British guest asks whether his “one-ounce” cartridges are legal at a FITASC event. What do you know?
  1. The FITASC limit is 28 g of lead (+2%), which is about one ounce
  2. FITASC allows 36 g
  3. Any load is allowed
  4. Only 24 g is allowed
Correct!

The FITASC limit is 28 g of lead (+2%), which is about one ounce — The rules allow up to 28 g of lead with a +2% tolerance; one ounce is about 28 g. Shot size (2.0–2.5 mm) must also fit.
