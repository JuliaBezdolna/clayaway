History of the sport · 1/5 · 5 min

From live birds to clay targets

Modern clay shooting grew out of hunting. Hunters wanted to practise out of season and to compete with each other under fair and equal conditions. In the 18th and 19th centuries, especially in England, they did this by shooting live pigeons released from small boxes placed in front of the shooter.

That era left words we still use today. The throwing machine is still called a trap in English, which was the name of the box holding the bird. And the call «pull», which the shooter gives before a target, is commonly linked to the string that was pulled to open the box.

Live-bird shooting drew more and more criticism, and the birds were expensive and flew differently every time. So in the 1860s an alternative appeared: glass balls. They were thrown by a special device and shattered when hit. From around 1866 this kind of shooting became hugely popular in the USA and drew big crowds.

Glass balls had a serious drawback: after every competition the field was covered in sharp splinters of glass. The real breakthrough came in 1880, when George Ligowsky of Cincinnati, USA, patented a flat, saucer-shaped target made of fired clay. Hence the name clay pigeon.

Modern targets have long stopped being made of clay: they are mostly a mixture of pitch and limestone, and biodegradable versions exist too. But the name stuck, and the whole sport is called clay shooting.

Over time live-bird shooting disappeared or was banned in many countries; in Great Britain, for example, it was banned by law in 1921. Today no live animals are shot in FITASC disciplines at all, and the Sporting™ rules put people's safety and care for the environment first.

Key points
  • Clay shooting grew out of hunting and live-pigeon shooting
  • Glass balls appeared as targets in the 1860s
  • In 1880 George Ligowsky patented the clay target, the clay pigeon
  • The words trap and «pull» come from those times
  • Modern targets are made of pitch and limestone, and no live animals are shot in FITASC disciplines

Did you know? Although modern targets are no longer made of clay, in English they are still called clay pigeons.
