Managing recoil

Many women give up shooting after the first taster session because «it kicks so hard». Usually the culprit is not recoil as such but a gun that does not fit, cartridges that are too heavy or a poor mount. All of these can be fixed.

Start with fit. When a stock of the right length sits in the shoulder pocket and the cheek rests firmly on the comb, gun and body move together and recoil spreads through the muscles instead of hitting one spot. Bruises on the cheek or shoulder are a sign the gun needs checking.

Stance: weight slightly on the front foot, body leaning a little forward, shoulders relaxed. Leaning back lets the gun push you even harder. Press the stock firmly into the shoulder: a gap between shoulder and stock makes the blow sharper.

Cartridges. FITASC rules allow loads up to 28 grams, but at normal distances most targets break convincingly with lighter loads such as 24 or 21 grams. There are also special low-recoil cartridges. They are ideal for learning.

The gun. A gas-operated semi-automatic uses part of the shot's energy to reload and spreads the push over time, so it feels softer. A heavier gun also recoils more softly but tires the arms — find your balance. A recoil pad and a shoulder pad in your vest help further.

And most important — do not grit your teeth. If your shoulder hurts, take a break or change cartridges. Pain quickly builds the habit of jerking before the shot (flinch), which is much harder to lose than a bruise. A good coach will set your workload so that shooting stays enjoyable.

Key points
  • Recoil is about fit and technique, not strength
  • Stock in the shoulder pocket, weight on the front foot
  • For learning — lighter loads (24 or 21 g) or low-recoil cartridges
  • A gas-operated semi-automatic feels softer
  • If it hurts — take a break; pain builds a flinch

Did you know? Recoil depends on the mass of the shot and its velocity, so going from 28 to 24 grams already noticeably reduces the push — and for most training targets that is plenty.
