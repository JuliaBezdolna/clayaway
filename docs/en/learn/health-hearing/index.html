Hearing: how to keep it

A shotgun shot is very loud: measurements vary, but the peak level at the shooter's ear is roughly 150–160 decibels (dB). For comparison, impulse noise above 140 dB is already considered able to damage hearing even with a single sound. And a day at the ground means hundreds of such shots, your own and other people's.

Noise-induced hearing loss builds up gradually and painlessly. Tiny sensitive cells in the inner ear die, and they do not grow back. At first a person struggles to follow speech in a noisy place, then stops hearing high sounds. What is lost cannot be restored — hearing can only be protected.

A common first warning is ringing or buzzing in the ears (tinnitus) after shooting. If your ears ring after training, your protection was not enough. Do not wait for it to pass by itself — change your protection, and if the ringing does not go away within a day or two or keeps coming back, see an ear, nose and throat (ENT) doctor or an audiologist.

That is why FITASC rules require hearing protection for everyone on or near a stand: shooters, referees, staff and spectators. In Parcours de Chasse – Sporting™ and Compak® Sporting a shooter without hearing protection is treated as absent and does not shoot.

There are three main kinds of protection: earplugs, passive ear muffs and electronic ear muffs, which let quiet speech through but damp the shot. The packaging states how much the product reduces noise; in Europe this is given as the SNR value in decibels.

Double hearing protection means earplugs and muffs at the same time. Their ratings do not simply add up, but together they give a noticeable extra margin. Double protection is especially recommended for children, for people who shoot a lot and on covered or enclosed stands, where sound bounces off walls and roofs.

Protection works only when fitted properly. Earplugs go in deep enough to seal the ear canal; glasses arms or a hat must not break the seal of the muffs. The best protection is custom earplugs moulded to your ear. This is general information, not medical advice: for questions about your own health, see a doctor.

Key points
  • A shot is roughly 150–160 dB; noise damage adds up and does not heal
  • Ringing ears after shooting means the protection was too weak
  • Hearing protection is compulsory for everyone near a stand
  • Double protection — plugs and muffs together — adds a margin
  • Persistent tinnitus is a reason to see an ENT doctor

Did you know? Electronic ear muffs often let you hear better than with none: they amplify the quiet voices of the referee and other shooters and damp the shot instantly.
