Lead: why you shoot in front of the target

A target moves, and the shot needs time to reach it. If you fire straight at the target, it will have moved on while the shot is flying, and the charge passes behind. So you shoot slightly in front: that is lead. The faster and further the target, and the bigger the angle (as with a crosser), the more lead it needs. A target coming straight at you or going straight away needs very little. This applies to all disciplines, including Parcours de Chasse – Sporting™ and Compak® Sporting.

Lead is not worth measuring in metres: different people see it differently, because each moves the gun at their own speed. That is why coaches teach a method, a way of getting the barrels in front of the target. There are three best-known methods.

Maintained lead. The barrels start in front of the target and move at its speed, keeping the gap you need. You stay ahead of the target as you follow it and fire when the picture is right. The method works well on long, predictable targets.

Pull-away. The barrels join the target, on it or on its front edge, move with it, then smoothly pull ahead, and you fire. Many coaches start beginners with this method because it is easy to understand.

Swing-through. The barrels start behind the target, catch it up, pass through it, and the shot goes off as they come out in front. The movement is faster, so the lead often looks smaller than with the other methods.

No method is “more correct” than another: experienced shooters choose the method for each target. For a beginner the key is to learn one method with a coach rather than mixing them all. And with every method the gun keeps moving at the shot, which is the subject of the next lesson.

Key points
  • Lead is needed because the target moves while the shot flies.
  • More lead for fast, distant and crossing targets.
  • Maintained lead: the barrels stay in front of the target throughout.
  • Pull-away: barrels on the target, then ahead; swing-through: from behind, through and ahead.
  • Learn one method with a coach.

Did you know? Two shooters can break the same target and describe the lead completely differently: one “sees” a metre, the other half a metre. The difference is in how fast each moves the gun.
