Recoil, shoulder and neck

Recoil is the backward push of the gun when it fires. You cannot avoid it completely, but recoil taken properly hardly hurts. If after a Parcours de Chasse – Sporting™ or Compak® Sporting session you have bruises on your shoulder, cheek or arm, or your neck aches, it is a sign something is wrong.

The most common cause is a poor gun mount. The stock should sit in the «pocket» of the shoulder between the collarbone and the shoulder joint, and the cheek should rest firmly on the comb. If the stock sits on bone or on the arm, or there is a gap between shoulder and stock, recoil hits sharply and painfully.

The second cause is a gun that does not fit. A stock that is too long or too short, or a comb that is too high or too low, makes you stretch your head, tense your neck and place the stock wrongly. Having the gun fitted (gun fit) by a coach or a gun fitter often removes pain faster than anything else.

The third is cartridges. FITASC rules allow loads up to 28 grams of shot, but in training you can use lighter loads such as 24 or 21 grams, or special low-recoil cartridges. At normal distances they break targets just as convincingly, and the shoulder tires far less.

The weight and design of the gun help too: by the laws of physics a heavier gun moves back more slowly, and a gas-operated semi-automatic spreads the push over a longer time. A recoil pad on the stock and a shoulder pad in your vest soften the blow.

The neck suffers when a shooter drops the head too low or reaches for the stock. The head should tilt only slightly, and the stock should come up to the face. A light neck and shoulder warm-up before shooting and between rounds also reduces tension.

Pain that does not go away within a few days, numbness, tingling in the arm or severe neck pain is a reason to see a doctor or physiotherapist, not to «shoot through it». This is general information, not medical advice: for questions about your own health, see a doctor.

Key points
  • Bruises and pain from recoil are a sign of a problem, not «normal»
  • Stock in the shoulder pocket, cheek firmly on the comb
  • Gun fitting often removes the pain
  • In training — lighter loads (24 or 21 g) or low-recoil cartridges
  • Pain that persists or numbness — see a doctor

Did you know? The habit of jerking before the shot in anticipation of recoil is called a flinch; it often starts precisely because of pain from a poorly fitted gun or overly heavy cartridges.
