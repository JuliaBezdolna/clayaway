Concentration and focus

A round of 25 targets in Parcours de Chasse – Sporting™ or Compak® Sporting takes longer than you think: walking between stands, waiting for your turn, watching targets being shown. The shooting itself is only a few seconds per target. Keeping maximum attention all that time is impossible, and you do not need to.

It helps to think of concentration as a switch. Between shots you rest: chat, look at the view, drink water. When you step onto the stand you switch your attention on; when you have finished you relax again.

While the targets are shown (target presentation) your attention scouts: where the target comes from, where you see it best, where you will break it. That gives you the point where you first see the target (pick-up point), the point where you hold the gun (hold point) and the point where you shoot (break point).

Before you call, your eyes are in soft focus: you look calmly at the area where the target will appear without staring at details. As soon as the target appears, your gaze switches to hard focus: you see the target itself sharply, ideally its leading edge, not the barrels or the bead.

What steals your attention is usually not the target but your thoughts: «what if I miss», «how many zeros do I have», «what will the coach say». A process goal helps — one simple action you think about on every target, such as «see the leading edge». Let the referee keep the score.

Concentration can be trained: short rounds with a clear routine, eye exercises (following birds or cars without moving your head), calm breathing. If your attention drifts after a few rounds, that is normal tiredness, and a break is better than forcing yourself to shoot.

Key points
  • Attention is a switch: turn it on at the stand, rest between shots
  • Target presentation is the time to choose pick-up, hold and break points
  • Soft focus before the call, hard focus on the target once it appears
  • One process goal instead of thoughts about the score

Did you know? Targets fly fast, but the human eye follows a moving object better than a still bead, which is why experienced shooters hardly notice the barrels during the shot.
