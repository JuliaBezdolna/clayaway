Documents and licences

This lesson is not legal advice. Laws on minors and guns are different in every country and change from time to time. The only reliable advice: check local law and the club's rules, and if needed ask the police authority that issues permits or the national federation.

In general you will meet different approaches. In some countries a child may shoot at a licensed ground without a permit of their own, but only under the direct supervision of a licensed adult or an instructor, and with a club gun or that adult's gun. Elsewhere there are youth permits from a certain age. Owning a gun of your own is usually possible only once you are an adult; until then it is registered to an adult.

Clubs usually ask for written consent from the parents or guardians (guardian consent), and sometimes also club membership, insurance and a medical certificate. Ask about this in advance so that you are not sorting it out next to the stand on the first day.

Competitions often require a sports licence from the national federation. This is not a gun permit but proof that the shooter is a federation member and may take part in its competitions. The club or the federation will tell you how to get one for a child.

Travelling abroad with a young shooter is a separate story. You may need parental consent for the child to travel, and the rules for minors in the destination country may differ from yours. Whether a European Firearms Pass is issued to a minor, and how a gun is entered in it, is decided by the country of residence. Ask long before the trip.

Keep copies of all documents in one folder and check expiry dates before each season.

Key points
  • Laws on minors and guns differ in every country — check local ones
  • A club usually asks for written parental consent
  • A federation sports licence is not a gun permit
  • Plan trips abroad with a child well ahead

Did you know? The European Firearms Pass is issued by the owner's country of residence and lists the specific guns they may travel with.
