How to watch clay shooting · 1/4 · 5 min

What to look at: targets, doubles and the shooter's routine

To a beginner every shot looks the same at first: a target flies, a shot, pieces. But an experienced spectator sees much more. In Parcours de Chasse – Sporting™ and Compak® Sporting each target is a small puzzle, and it is interesting to watch how the shooter solves it.

Start with the target type. Most often it is a standard target. There are smaller ones too, midi and mini, which fly faster and look further away. A battue is a thin flat target that turns on its edge in flight. A rabbit is a thick disc that rolls and bounces along the ground like a hare.

Next, the trajectory. A target may fly towards the shooter (incomer), away (going away), across the field (crosser), climb almost vertically (teal) or follow an arc (looper). Try to name the trajectory before the shot — it is a great game for a spectator.

Watch doubles separately, that is, two targets. In a double on report the second target is released at the moment of the first shot. In a simultaneous double both targets are released together. Sporting™ also has the rafale: two targets from the same trap on the same trajectory, one after the other.

Finally, the shooter's routine (pre-shot routine). Before each target a good shooter does the same thing: sets the feet, looks at where the target will appear and where they will break it, takes the ready position and calls «pull». In Sporting™ the gun butt must be below the shoulder until the target appears, so you can clearly see how smoothly the shooter mounts the gun.

Listen to the referee too. If the target is missed, the referee announces «zero» or otherwise makes clear that it is lost. That way you learn to keep score along with the referee.

Key points
  • Tell the targets apart: standard, midi, mini, battue, rabbit
  • Name the trajectory before the shot
  • Doubles are on report, simultaneous and rafale (in Sporting™)
  • Watch the shooter's routine and listen to the referee's calls

Did you know? A battue turns on its edge in flight and for a moment becomes almost invisible — which is why it is so interesting to watch.
